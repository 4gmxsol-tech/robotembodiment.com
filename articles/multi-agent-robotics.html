Multi-Agent Robotics: Coordination, Communication and Planning

A multi-agent robotic system contains two or more autonomous or semi-autonomous agents whose actions affect one another. The agents may cooperate, compete for resources or operate independently in a shared environment. Designing such systems requires coordination across task planning, motion, communication and safety.

Research guide · November 5, 2026 · Robot Embodiment Editorial

The multi-agent problem

Each robot has partial observations, limited resources and its own dynamics. A locally reasonable action can be globally harmful: two robots may select the same object, block a corridor or execute incompatible plans. Multi-agent systems must therefore reason about other agents' capabilities, intentions and likely future states.

Task allocation and role assignment

Task allocation maps goals to agents. Methods range from centralized optimization and market-based auctions to distributed consensus and learned assignment policies. Relevant constraints include payload, reachability, tool availability, battery, deadlines, uncertainty and the cost of switching tasks. Reassignment is important when a robot fails or the environment changes.

Communication is a resource

Communication can support shared maps, intent exchange, negotiation and synchronized execution. But bandwidth, latency, packet loss, security and network partitions limit what can be assumed. Robust systems should degrade gracefully: safety-critical local behavior must continue even when fleet coordination is unavailable. Messages need clear semantics and timestamps so agents can distinguish current facts from stale plans.

Planning at multiple levels

High-level planners allocate goals and sequence subtasks. Mid-level planners coordinate routes and avoid conflicts. Low-level controllers stabilize motion and manage contact. A layered architecture can reduce complexity, but abstractions must expose constraints: a task planner should know when a grasp is infeasible or a route is blocked. Replanning should be triggered by meaningful state changes, not every noisy observation.

Learning-based coordination

Multi-agent reinforcement learning, imitation learning and language-model-assisted planning can help agents develop coordination policies. Yet training can be expensive and brittle under changes in team size, communication topology or robot morphology. Evaluate policies under unseen team compositions and communication failures. A language model may help interpret goals or negotiate plans, but it should not replace verified motion and safety controllers.

Safety, accountability and evaluation

Measure collision risk, deadlocks, task success, time and energy, fairness, communication cost and recovery after failures. Define right-of-way rules, safe separation, emergency stop behavior and human override. Record which agent made each decision and what information was available. In mixed human-robot environments, safety constraints must be enforced locally and independently of high-level reasoning.
