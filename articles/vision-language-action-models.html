Vision-Language-Action Models for Robots

VLA models connect what a robot sees, what a person asks and what the robot can physically do.

Published September 26, 2026 · Updated November 18, 2026 · Robot Embodiment Editorial
Written and reviewed by the Robot Embodiment Editorial team. Claims are linked to primary research and official sources where practical — see our editorial policy.

What does VLA mean?

Vision-language-action describes a model or system that connects visual observations and language instructions to physical actions. A VLA approach seeks a shared pathway from multimodal context to behavior.

A simple pipeline

A typical system can receive images or video, a natural-language goal and robot state. It then predicts an action representation, trajectory or intermediate command that a lower-level controller executes.

How VLA models are built

Most VLA systems start from a pretrained vision-language model and add an action prediction head. A vision encoder reads camera images, a language encoder reads the instruction, and a transformer fuses the two streams and predicts what the robot should do next. The pretrained model contributes broad knowledge about objects, scenes and language; robot training data teaches it how that knowledge maps onto physical action.

There are two main ways to represent actions. Token-based systems, such as RT-2 and OpenVLA, discretize continuous robot motions into tokens, so the model predicts actions much as a language model predicts words. This reuses the machinery of large pretrained models, but it quantizes smooth motion. Continuous systems instead generate actions directly: π0, developed by Physical Intelligence, uses flow matching to produce smooth action trajectories, an approach related to diffusion-based control. Continuous generation suits high-frequency, dexterous motion; tokenization suits reasoning-heavy tasks where the model benefits from its language training.

In practice, a VLA rarely drives motors directly. It typically outputs end-effector targets, waypoints or short action chunks at a few hertz, while a lower-level controller executes them at hundreds of hertz, handling contact, compliance and safety limits. The division of labor matters: the VLA supplies semantic understanding and planning, and the controller supplies the fast feedback loop that keeps motion stable.

Major VLA models

RT-2 (Google DeepMind, 2023) was the first large-scale demonstration that web-scale vision-language knowledge could transfer to robot control. It showed that a model trained on internet data could interpret novel instructions and objects on a robot, though its control remained coarse. RT-2 established the VLA category and the research agenda that followed.

OpenVLA (2024) is an open 7-billion-parameter VLA trained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset of robot demonstrations. Because its weights and training recipe are public, it became a common starting point for research systems and fine-tuning experiments. It showed that an open model could match or approach closed systems on standard manipulation benchmarks, and it made VLA research reproducible outside large labs.

π0 (Physical Intelligence, 2024) took the continuous route: a flow-matching VLA trained across multiple robots and tasks, designed for general robot control rather than a single arm. Its successor work pushed toward open-world generalization, handling new objects and environments without task-specific retraining. Read our π0 model profile and the Physical Intelligence company profile.

Gemini Robotics 2 (Google DeepMind) extends the VLA idea across embodiments, including humanoids, with capabilities spanning dexterity, whole-body control and multi-robot collaboration. It is the clearest example of the field's direction: one model family intended to control different bodies rather than a separate policy per robot. See also the Google DeepMind company profile.

Figure's Helix is a VLA model built specifically for humanoid control, running on the company's Figure 03 robot. Helix shows the other end of the spectrum from general research models: a system co-designed with one body, its cameras and its hands, where embodiment-specific tuning is a feature rather than a limitation.

Why language helps

Language provides a compact interface for goals such as locating an object, changing a task or specifying a constraint. But language alone cannot solve embodiment: the model still needs grounding in the target robot's sensors, capabilities and action interface.

The embodiment challenge

A VLA model trained on one action representation may not transfer directly to another. Different robots expose different joints, grippers, cameras and control frequencies. Practical VLA systems therefore need action grounding, adaptation or embodiment-specific interfaces.

Where VLA models run

The same model family now appears on very different bodies. Robot arms use VLAs for pick-and-place, packing and assembly; mobile manipulators add navigation to the problem; humanoids such as Atlas from Boston Dynamics and Figure 03 must coordinate locomotion, balance and manipulation at the same time. Each body changes the action space, the sensors and the failure modes, so a VLA that works on one platform is not automatically portable to another. That portability question is the subject of our guide to cross-embodiment learning.

Data: the bottleneck

Robot data is scarce compared with the text and images used to train language and vision models. Collecting it is slow and expensive: demonstrations require teleoperation, human video or autonomous interaction, and every hour of data is tied to a specific robot, camera setup and task. The Open X-Embodiment project pooled demonstrations from dozens of datasets and robot types to give VLA training a shared starting point, but the total remains orders of magnitude smaller than web-scale data. This is why physical robot data matters so much, and why learning from human demonstrations — including ordinary video of people doing tasks — is a major research direction. Simulation helps, but only if the sim-to-real gap is managed; see our sim-to-real robotics guide.

How to judge a VLA model

A demonstration video is not evidence. Useful evaluation asks whether the system succeeds across repeated trials, generalizes to new objects, instructions and environments, recovers from disturbances, and responds fast enough for real control. Latency matters: a model that reasons beautifully but acts at one hertz cannot track a moving object or correct a slipping grasp. Safety matters too — a VLA that misinterprets an instruction in a shared workspace can apply force in the wrong place. Benchmark scores are meaningful only when the setup, baselines and failure conditions are documented; our robot embodiment benchmark guide explains what to look for, and what embodied AI means frames the broader evaluation problem.

Where VLA systems fit

  • Instruction following for manipulation
  • Long-horizon task execution
  • Human-robot interaction
  • Generalist robot policies
  • Transfer across tasks and potentially embodiments

What to watch

For physical AI, the important question is whether behavior remains reliable when the environment, task, sensor configuration or robot body changes. A VLA that only works in its training lab is a prototype; one that transfers across bodies — from an arm to a humanoid such as Atlas — is a step toward general physical intelligence.
