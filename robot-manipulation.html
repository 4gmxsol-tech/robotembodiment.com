Robotic manipulation

Robotic manipulation enables robots to grasp, move, place, assemble and interact with objects through perception, planning, contact and feedback.

Read the full robotic manipulation guide →

The manipulation loop

1. Perceive

Estimate objects, surfaces, pose, contact opportunities and uncertainty using the sensors available on the robot.

2. Plan

Select grasps, motions and task sequences while accounting for geometry, reachability, collision risk and physical constraints.

3. Control

Execute motion and force commands while responding to contact, disturbances and changes in the environment.

Contact-aware execution

Manipulation differs from free-space movement because the robot must reason about contact. Friction, compliance, object mass and small alignment errors can change the result of an action. Vision helps estimate object pose, while force, tactile or proprioceptive signals can help detect contact and slipping when those sensors are available.

Reliable systems close the loop: they observe whether the grasp, placement or insertion succeeded, then correct or re-plan when the physical result differs from the intended state. Compliance can help with small alignment errors, but it does not replace explicit force, speed and workspace limits.

How to evaluate a manipulation system

Evaluate repeated trials under stated conditions rather than relying on a single successful demonstration. Useful measures include task success rate, completion time, grasp stability, recovery after disturbances, operator interventions and safety events. Report the robot, end effector, sensors, objects, environment and retry rules so results can be interpreted and compared.

Dexterity and embodiment

Dexterous manipulation uses richer contact patterns and more capable hands or end effectors. It is sensitive to sensing, control bandwidth, data quality and the robot's morphology. A skill that works on one arm or hand may require retargeting, calibration or adaptation before it transfers to another embodiment.
